Every Recipe. Four Numbers.

01

Ingredient Cost

What the amounts in the recipe cost, pro rated from the package.

If a 900 g bag costs $4.00 and a recipe uses 450 g, the ingredient cost is $2.00.

02

Batch Total

The combined cost of all ingredients actually used: the whole pot, pan, or tray.

03

Cost Per Serving

The batch total divided by the tested yield. Servings are real portions, the size a hungry adult eats, and the yield is stated on the recipe.

$11.52 ÷ 6 = $1.92

04

Basket Cost

What you pay at the till if you need every package on the list. A recipe can cost $1.34 a serving and still need a $30 basket the first time, and we would rather show you both numbers than surprise you at the store.

The Sources

Source A

Government Price Data

The backbone is Statistics Canada Table 18-10-0245, monthly average retail prices for selected products, published for Canada and for each province.

It covers the staples this site cooks with: lentils and dry beans, potatoes, carrots, onions, cabbage, rice, pasta, eggs, milk, cheese, canned tomatoes, frozen vegetables, and the common cuts of chicken, pork, and beef. The table appears on Statistics Canada's own publication schedule, so its reference month can differ from the date of an individual retail check.

Statistics Canada Table 18-10-0245 ↗

Source B

Grocery Catalogue Checks

Where the table does not list an item, we price it from the current regular price in a national chain's public online catalogue, and say so on the recipe.

Store brands are the default buy: No Name, President's Choice, Compliments, Great Value, and their peers. Figures are Canadian dollars, before tax.

Three Dates, Three Meanings

Government Reference Month

The month the Statistics Canada table describes.

Retail Check Date

When a catalogue price was checked for an item the table does not cover, noted on the recipe.

Recipe Prices Checked

The month printed on each recipe, when its pricing was last reviewed.

The Rules We Follow

01

Regular Prices First

Regular prices carry the headline. A sale can be mentioned, and a recipe built around a sale is labelled sale priced with the regular price result shown too. A number that only works during one flyer week is not a budget recipe.

02

Show the Range

Every figure carries a range. A typical low to high range sits beside the headline price, built from the provincial figures and package choices in the same sources. National averages are a planning tool, not a promise about one store on one day.

03

Date Every Price

Sources and the month checked are printed on the recipe. Prices are rechecked on a rhythm: the most visited recipes monthly, the rest quarterly, oldest first. When a recheck moves a recipe to a different price tier, the recipe gets a visible update note with the new date and the new cost per serving.

04

Account for Pantry Items

Small pantry items are an allowance, and it is labelled. Salt, pepper, dried spices, and garlic are costed as a small flat allowance per recipe rather than pretending a teaspoon of paprika has a knowable price. The allowance is in the breakdown where you can see it.

05

Real Servings

A serving is never shrunk to hit a number. A recipe that misses its tier gets a cheaper protein, a different package, or a seasonal swap, or it is relabelled at the honest tier.

What These Prices Aren't

They are not a live shelf price for your store, and they are not a guarantee. They are a careful, sourced estimate that tells you what a recipe should cost within a known range, so you can plan a week of dinners without doing arithmetic in the aisle. If your store beats the range, that is a good day. Tell us, and the range gets better.
